Lesson 1

What a credit score actually is

About 15 minutes  ·  140 points
Short win: You stop fearing the number and start understanding it. That shift alone is worth the lesson.

A credit score is not a judgment of your character or your reliability as a person. It is a number that responds to specific behaviours in a specific sequence.

How it works in Canada:

  • Your score is calculated by two credit bureaus: Equifax Canada and TransUnion Canada
  • Scores generally run from 300 to 900
  • Lenders use it as one input alongside income, debts, and history
  • A score of 680 or above qualifies you for the best insured mortgage rates in Canada

Here is the freeing part: because the score responds to behaviours, it can be changed by changing those behaviours. A score is not a fixed mark. It moves. People rebuild scores all the time, in a known sequence, on a realistic timeline.

And you have something conventional first-time buyers racing to buy never have: time. Years of runway to build a credit profile that makes your Year 10 mortgage application straightforward.

Check your score now, free at both Borrowell (Equifax) and Credit Karma Canada (TransUnion). Using either will not affect your score, it is a soft inquiry. Monitoring both bureaus gives you the fullest picture because they can sometimes differ.

Worked example

Sam had avoided looking at his score for years because he assumed it would confirm something bad. When he finally checked, it was 648. Not where he wanted it, but far from a disaster, and entirely workable with years of runway ahead. The relief of simply knowing was bigger than he expected.

Your action step

Check your credit score for free at Borrowell or Credit Karma Canada. Write down the number.

Lesson 2

The five factors and what they mean

About 15 minutes  ·  150 points
Short win: You know exactly what moves your score, so improvement is no longer guesswork.

Your credit score is built from five factors. Knowing their approximate weight tells you where to focus.

The five factors, in order of influence:

  • Payment history (about 35 percent): do you pay on time? The single most powerful thing you can do. One missed payment can hurt. Years of clean payments help enormously
  • Credit utilisation (about 30 percent): how much of your available credit you are using. Keep it below 30 percent of your total limit. This is the fastest factor to improve
  • Length of credit history (about 15 percent): how long your accounts have been open. Keeping older accounts open helps, even if you rarely use them
  • Credit mix (about 10 percent): having a variety of credit types (card, line of credit, loan) shows you can manage different obligations
  • New inquiries (about 10 percent): applying for multiple credit products in a short window can signal financial stress. Space out applications

Payment history and utilisation together make up roughly two-thirds of your score. Both can be actively improved. That is where most people should start.

Worked example

Sam looked at his five factors. His payment history was strong, he always paid on time. His length of history was fine. But his utilisation was high: he was carrying balances near his card limits. That one factor was dragging his score down more than anything else, and it was also the fastest to fix.

Your action step

Review your five credit factors in your report. Identify which one is your weakest right now and write it down.

Lesson 3

The utilisation ratio, your fastest win

About 15 minutes  ·  150 points
Short win: You learn the single fastest lever to move your score upward, sometimes within one or two billing cycles.

Of all five factors, utilisation responds the fastest to action, which makes it the best place to start for most people.

How utilisation works:

  • It is the share of your available revolving credit that you are currently using
  • If you have a $10,000 total limit and are carrying $4,000 in balances, your utilisation is 40 percent
  • The widely used guideline is to keep it below 30 percent
  • Utilisation resets every billing cycle. Unlike payment history, which takes time to build, lowering utilisation can move your score within weeks

Three ways to lower it, you can use more than one:

  • Pay down balances, most direct
  • Make a payment partway through the billing cycle rather than only at the due date, so the reported balance is lower
  • Ask for a credit limit increase without increasing spending. Raises the denominator and lowers the ratio. Only do this if you trust yourself not to spend the new room
Worked example

Sam was using $4,200 of a $6,000 limit, 70 percent utilisation, hurting his score badly. He used three months of budget surplus to bring the balance to $1,500, dropping utilisation to 25 percent. Within two billing cycles his score had risen by more than 40 points from that one change alone.

Your action step

Calculate your current utilisation ratio: total balances divided by total credit limits. If it is above 30 percent, make a plan to bring it down.

Lesson 4

Your 12-month improvement plan

About 15 minutes  ·  150 points
Short win: You leave with a realistic, sequenced plan, not vague intentions.

Improving a credit score follows a sequence.

A realistic 12-month sequence:

  • Months 1 to 3: Stop the bleeding. Make every payment on time, automate minimums so nothing slips, stop applying for new credit
  • Months 4 to 6: Attack utilisation. Direct your budget surplus at your highest-utilisation card until it is below 30 percent. This is where most of your early score improvements come from
  • Months 7 to 9: Hold the line. Keep paying on time, keep utilisation low, resist closing your oldest card, its age is helping your history
  • Months 10 to 12: Review and correct errors. Check your credit report at both bureaus. Dispute any errors, they are more common than people expect and disputing is free. Note your progress

Twelve months of this sequence is realistic, not optimistic. And you are not doing it under deadline pressure. You have years of building period ahead. If high-interest debt is slowing you down, the Credit Counselling Society's Debt Repayment Calculator shows you the fastest payoff sequence for free.

Worked example

Sam started at 648. He automated his payments, brought his utilisation from 70 percent to 24 percent over four months, left his old card open, and disputed one error on his report. Eleven months later he was at 712, comfortably into the range that supports a strong mortgage application, with years still ahead.

Your action step

Write down your current score and your 12-month target. Write your first sequenced step, the single highest-priority action to take this month.

Lesson 5

Credit and your Year 10 mortgage application

About 14 minutes  ·  160 points
Short win: You see how ten years of small credit habits connect directly to the moment that completes your pathway.

Here is where credit connects to the whole reason you are in this course. At Year 10, you will apply for a mortgage to complete your purchase at the agreed price. Your credit profile is central to that application, and you have a decade to build one.

What a lender will look for:

  • Your score, ideally 680 or above for best insured rates
  • Your payment history: years of clean payments on every obligation
  • Your debt levels: GDS (housing cost as a percentage of gross income) below 39 percent, TDS (all debt as a percentage of gross income) below 44 percent
  • Your track record of managing credit responsibly over time

Here is what makes HOPE participants different from every other first-time buyer a broker will see: you arrive at Year 10 with a decade of documented, on-time housing payments. That is not just a good credit score, that is a proven track record. A conventional buyer scraping together a deposit has a few years of history. You have ten.

Keep track of your credit score throughout your building period. We will remind you of this at your annual check-in and guide you through how to do it.

Worked example

By Year 9, Sam's score sat at 760 and he had nine years of on-time occupancy contributions behind him. When he sat down with his mortgage broker, the conversation was short. The file spoke for itself. The work he had done in Year 1 was what made Year 10 feel calm.

Your action step

Note your current credit score to bring to your next HOPE check-in. Set a reminder to check and note it throughout your building period.
